Good afternoon. We are Wed_team3. Our topic sits in Layer 3 of the AI technology stack: data and data infrastructure. Scale AI and Appen both sell AI training data. In 2024 the market grew 25%, yet Scale AI grew about 14.5% while Appen shrank 14.2%. Our question: why did two firms in the same growing industry end up so differently? We answer it in three steps: how AI changed the industry, how each firm's resources and organization fit that change, and what each firm should do next.

This is the core of our external story. AI affects the industry in two directions at once. First, it creates demand: LLM developers need RLHF, expert-written data, model evaluation and red-teaming. That pushed the market to $3.188 billion in 2024, up 25.2%. Second, AI substitutes for the old work: auto-labeling, synthetic data and self-supervised learning reduce the need for routine manual labeling. Both branches lead to one result: the key success factors move away from labor scale and low cost toward expertise, technology, data quality and organizational capability. Keep these four factors in mind; we use them to compare the firms later.

Market growth does not make the industry easy. In FY2024, buyer power and rivalry were strong, entry was moderate to strong, suppliers and substitutes were moderate, so overall attractiveness is medium. Looking ahead to 2025–2029, three dynamics come from AI: substitution gets stronger, experts gain bargaining power, and buyers stay powerful because Big Tech builds in-house. Regulation adds pressure: data sovereignty, gig-labor rules and privacy law all raise costs for cross-border crowd models. So profits go to firms with technology, expertise and quality, not to firms with the biggest crowd.

Here is the outcome we need to explain. Scale AI's revenue, based on third-party estimates, rose from about 100 million dollars in 2020 to about 870 million in 2024. Appen peaked in 2021 at 447 million and fell to 234 million. In 2024 the industry grew 25%, Scale about 14.5% and Appen fell 14.2%. Two cautions. Now the internal analysis, the heart of our argument. Using Porter's value chain, the biggest differences are in four outlined activities. In operations, Scale combines software, automation and expert verification; Appen relies on a crowd and legacy workflows. In service, Scale sells continuous model improvement through evaluation and red-teaming, which creates repeat business; Appen is more project-based. VRIO asks whether a resource is valuable, rare, costly to imitate, and whether the firm is organized to capture its value. Scale's RLHF and model evaluation capability meets all four: a sustained advantage. Its workforce and client relationships are valuable but imitable or only partly rare, so at most temporary. Appen's multilingual and speech data is valuable and partly rare, but look at the O column: only partially met. After losing Google, Appen itself said it needed to improve delivery, crowd management, leadership and sales. Traditional crowdsourcing, the old core, now gives only parity. So the decisive column is O. The next question is why Appen could not organize around its assets.

Why did one firm adapt and the other struggle? Scale AI shows dynamic capabilities: it started with autonomous-driving data, moved into generative AI and RLHF, and by 2024 was doing model evaluation and government AI testing. Each time it sensed new demand and reconfigured its resources. Appen shows the opposite pattern, core rigidity. Its global crowd was the reason for its success, and the operating model built around volume made the move to expert tasks hard. But be careful: the network itself is still valuable. The rigidity is in how the work is organized around it. Relative to Slide 2, Scale moved toward the new key success factors; Appen's configuration is still built for the old one.

The Congruence Model explains the O in VRIO. It checks whether strategy, critical tasks, people, culture, formal organization and leadership fit together. Scale AI shows high congruence: a differentiation strategy, expert RLHF tasks, specialist contributors, a meritocratic culture, an integrated platform, and a founder-CEO driving all of it in FY2024. Scale AI's task is to sustain its advantage. Each recommendation comes from a finding. First, since RLHF and evaluation is its only capability meeting all four VRIO criteria and GenAI demand is growing, Scale should package model evaluation and red-teaming as recurring services and organize experts into dedicated domain pools. That keeps tasks, people and structure aligned. Second, the Congruence Model showed friction with contributors, and labor regulation is a threat. So Scale should review worker classification and pay, audit subcontractors, use experts only where expertise is needed, and automate routine work: lower legal risk and lower cost. Third, buyer power is strong and revenue depends on a few frontier labs. Scale should grow its existing government and defense work, win enterprise clients in other industries, and invest in data security and privacy to earn their trust.

Appen is our most important case, because it needs to rebuild. The key principle: Appen should not copy Scale AI. It should build on what it already has. First, the workforce. Core rigidity and the HR–task mismatch say Appen's crowd is not set up for expert work. With limited resources, building experts in-house or buying a firm is too slow or too costly, so Appen should borrow: non-equity alliances with universities and professional associations. It should also train existing contributors and create a tiered workforce, crowd for general tasks and experts for complex ones, with fair pay and realistic deadlines. Second, positioning. Appen is stuck in the middle but owns valuable multilingual resources, so it should focus on high-value multilingual LLM evaluation, reinvest its cost savings in technology, and measure managers on quality and high-value revenue, not just cost. Third, clients. The Google loss and strong buyer power show the danger of concentration. Appen should target mid-market, healthcare and finance, use in-country contributors for Sovereign AI projects, and move to recurring contracts through Quality Flow and Build My RAG. The evidence: one million contributors in 170 countries and 500 languages, 37 new customers in China, Japan and Korea in the first half of 2024, and non-Google revenue up 16%.

Macro Trends Favor Expert, Compliant Providers over Cross-Border Crowds

Political

  • AI techno-nationalism and data-sovereignty rules restrict offshore data and labor
  • Geopolitical labor risk in gig networks raises compliance costs
  • Government AI subsidies and public datasets (US, EU, Korea)

Economic

  • GenAI capital inflow drives expert data demand: market $3.188B, +25.2% (2024)
  • Cost pressure favors hybrid auto-label + human review
  • FX (Appen pays globally) and funding climate (Scale raised ≈$1B in 2024)

Socio-cultural

  • Concern over hallucination and bias raises the value of diverse, human-verified data
  • Fair-labor issues in the gig economy raise ESG risk

Technological

  • Shift to RLHF and subject-matter-expert labeling (coding, medicine, law)
  • Synthetic data and auto-labeling raise productivity but threaten manual labeling

Environmental

  • Data-center energy use from large multimodal datasets (limited direct impact)
  • Clients demand greener infrastructure and ESG compliance

Legal

  • Copyright suits (e.g., NYT v. OpenAI) require licensed, traceable pipelines
  • Unclear IP ownership of annotated data
  • GDPR / CCPA: PII de-identification and consent
Opportunities: RLHF and expert labeling, public AI investment, bias mitigation. Threats to crowd models: data sovereignty, labor regulation, synthetic data.

Appendix A9 · Analysis Limitations

No profitability comparison between the two firms is made.

Google's exit directly hit Appen

FY2024 scope
